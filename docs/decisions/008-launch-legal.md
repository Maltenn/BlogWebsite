## What OurSpace does today (from the repo)

These are facts about the code, not legal conclusions. I read `src/accounts/auth.js`, `src/shared/dates.js`, `src/browse/browse.js`, `src/safety/safety.js`, `public/js/pages/rules.js`, `public/js/widgets/auth-forms.js`, `docs/mobile/`, and the docs the task named.

- **Age check.** Sign-up takes a birthday in a plain date field with no default. Under `MIN_AGE` (default 13, a Worker setting) it refuses with "sorry, you have to be N or older to join ourspace" and sets a 24-hour `os_agegate` cookie (HttpOnly, SameSite=Lax, path `/api/signup`) so the same browser cannot try another year (`src/accounts/auth.js`, `ageGate`). The birthday is stored. It is never shown; the profile may show age and star sign if the member allows it. A member cannot edit it: Settings says "if it's wrong, ask a moderator". There is a report reason "this person may be under the minimum age" (`src/catalog/options.js`) but no written procedure for what a moderator then does.
- **Teens and adults.** Browse (For you, Mutuals, Who to follow) never puts an adult and a 13-17-year-old in front of each other, using the birthdays. Boards, tag pages, profiles, search by name and **direct messages are not split**: the default `dm_policy` is `everyone` (`docs/architecture.md`), so an adult can open a DM to a 15-year-old unless the teen limits DMs to friends.
- **Photos.** Reviewed by hand in a moderation queue. Nothing scans for CSAM. Photos in chat are not built. A `csam` report on a post holds its photos.
- **Reports need a login.** `POST /api/reports` is a member route (`src/index.js`). There is no public contact address, no DMCA page, no Take It Down page, no terms of service. `/rules` is the only legal text.
- **Apps.** Capacitor shells around the same `public/` bundle (`mobile/package.json`). No age-API plugin is installed and nothing in `mobile/` or `src/` mentions the Apple or Google age APIs. The mobile contract says SameSite=Lax cookies do not travel from the app's origin to the API (`docs/mobile/contract.md`), so the 24-hour age cookie probably does not work in the apps. That is my inference; it needs a test.
- **No company.** No entity, revenue or ads. Cloudflare Workers, D1 and R2 host everything. Email goes through Resend, bot checks through Cloudflare Turnstile, and an AI-writing detector (off by default) may send public post text to a US vendor.

## 1. App-store age laws (TX, UT, LA, AL)

### How the four laws work, in plain English
All four say roughly this: the **app store** must learn each account holder's age category (child under 13, younger teen 13-15, older teen 16-17, adult), tie a minor's account to a parent account, and get a parent's consent before the minor downloads, buys or makes an in-app purchase. The store must then pass the age category and consent status to the **developer** when the developer asks. The **developer** must do things with that information. Developer duties are the part that falls on OurSpace.

### Texas SB 2420 (Business & Commerce Code ch. 121)
- **Developer duties.** Give each app store an age rating for the app and for each purchase, with the reasons (s. 121.052). Tell each app store before any "significant change" to the terms of service or privacy policy, defined as changing the type or category of personal data collected, stored or shared, changing the rating, adding new monetisation, or materially changing functionality or user experience (s. 121.053). "Create and implement a system to use information received from the app store" to verify each user's age category and whether a minor's parent consented (s. 121.054). Use that data only for age restrictions, legal compliance and safety defaults, and delete it after the verification (s. 121.055). **CONFIRMED** ([bill text](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/SB02420F.htm)).
- **Violations.** Enforcing a contract or terms of service against a minor without consent, knowingly misrepresenting the rating, or sharing the data (s. 121.056). A developer is not liable for the verification duty if it relied in good faith on the store's information and otherwise complied, and not for a rating violation if it used widely adopted standards in good faith. A violation is a deceptive trade practice "actionable under" DTPA subchapter E (s. 121.101). **CONFIRMED** (same source).
- **Size, revenue, free apps.** I found no threshold, no small-business exemption and no free-app exemption in the text. The only exemptions are from the *parental consent* step, for certain emergency-service and nonprofit-test apps. **CONFIRMED** (same source).
- **Status.** The district court enjoined the law before 2026-01-01; the Fifth Circuit stayed that injunction (administrative stay 2026-05-28; stay pending appeal 2026-06-04, per one tracker); the Supreme Court denied the application to vacate the stay on 2026-07-06, so the law is enforceable while the appeal goes on. Oral argument was reported for 2026-08-04, and I found no merits decision (as of a tracker checked through 2026-10-13). **CONFIRMED** for the Supreme Court denial ([docket 25A1390](https://www.supremecourt.gov/search.aspx?filename=/docket/docketfiles/html/public/25a1390.html)). **UNVERIFIED** for the Fifth Circuit dates and argument ([Texas data law tracker](https://texasdatalaw.com/tracker/sb-2420/), [InfoLawGroup](https://www.infolawgroup.com/insights/2026/7/7/supreme-court-clears-the-way-texass-app-store-accountability-act-is-now-enforceable)). Someone should check the Fifth Circuit docket (Nos. 25-51073 and 26-50001) the day before launch.
- **Sources disagree.** (a) The stay date is 2026-05-28 in one source and 2026-06-04 in another; the tracker explains the first was administrative. (b) InfoLawGroup says there is a private right of action; the statute only says a violation is a DTPA deceptive trade practice, which may or may not open the DTPA's consumer suit route. That is a question for the lawyer.

### Utah (SB 142 of 2025, amended by HB 498 of 2026; now Utah Code ch. 13-76)
- Original SB 142: developer duties from 2026-05-06, enforcement from 2026-12-31 (Division of Consumer Protection). **CONFIRMED** ([SB 142 enrolled](https://le.utah.gov/Session/2025/bills/enrolled/SB0142.pdf)).
- HB 498 moves the app-store and developer duties to **2027-05-06**; removes the state enforcement section; leaves a private right of action only for "a minor, or the parent of that minor, who has been harmed" by a developer enforcing a contract against a minor without verified parental consent, misrepresenting the consent disclosure, or sharing age category data; damages are the greater of actual damages or $1,000 per violation, plus fees. It says a developer "may request that an app store provider prevent minor accounts from downloading or purchasing" its app (s. 13-76-202(6)), and it drops the earlier duty to use the data to enforce its own age limits. It adds pre-installed apps, and the developer duties still include verifying age category and parental-consent status through the store and notifying the store of significant changes (here defined more narrowly than Texas: terms or privacy-policy changes that alter data categories, the age rating, or introduce in-app purchases or ads where there were none). **CONFIRMED** ([HB 498 enrolled](https://le.utah.gov/Session/2026/bills/enrolled/HB0498.pdf), text read).
- No size threshold or free-app exemption in either text. **CONFIRMED** (both read).
- HB 498's own effective-date clause depends on a two-thirds vote; I did not confirm the vote or signing date. Secondary sources say the amendments took effect early enough that the challenge was dismissed on 2026-04-21 ([Alston & Bird](https://www.alstonprivacy.com/challenge-to-utahs-app-store-accountability-act-voluntarily-dismissed-following-statutory-amendments/)). **UNVERIFIED.**
- **Sources disagree.** Apple's developer news of 2026-02-24 says age categories are shared for new Utah accounts from 2026-05-06 ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=f5zj08ey)); that matches the original SB 142, not HB 498, and the page I read does not mention the amendment. I take the enrolled bill as controlling. **CONFIRMED** for what each document says.

### Louisiana (HB 570 of 2025 replaced by HB 977, Act 185 of 2026)
- Act 185 says Act 481 (HB 570) "shall not become effective", and the replacement Part II of R.S. 51:1771-1775 takes effect **2027-07-01**. Developers must verify age category and parental consent through the store, notify the store of significant changes, enforce legal and self-set age limits, request data at download and on significant change, and may not sell age category data. The attorney general enforces, fines up to $10,000 per violation, with a 45-day written notice and chance to cure. A developer is not liable if it shows it complied with the developer section. No size threshold; the only carve-out is for "family account" apps that bar under-18s and are paid. **CONFIRMED** ([Act 185](https://legis.la.gov/Legis/ViewDocument.aspx?d=1475238), [bill page: signed 2026-05-15](https://legis.la.gov/legis/BillInfo.aspx?s=26RS&b=HB977&sbi=y)).
- Louisiana's developer section also keeps a separate duty for developers that must age-verify under R.S. 9:2800.29 (the state's social media law); I did not read that statute, so I cannot say whether OurSpace is covered. **UNVERIFIED.**
- **Sources disagree.** Apple's 2026-02-24 note says Louisiana from 2026-07-01 (older law). The enrolled Act 185 says 2027-07-01. The Act controls.

### Alabama (HB 161, 2026)
- Effective **2027-01-01**; accounts that existed on 2026-10-02 must be verified by 2027-10-01. Developer duties mirror Texas: verify age category and consent through the store, notify of significant changes, limit use of the data, use the **lowest** age category between the store's data and any age data the developer collected itself when setting age defaults, no sharing. Only the attorney general may sue, "knowing or reckless" violations only, up to $7,500 per violation, one-year limit. No size threshold. **CONFIRMED** ([enrolled HB 161](https://alison.legislature.state.al.us/files/pdf/SearchableInstruments/2026RS/HB161-enr.pdf)).
- Signing: reported as Act 2026-59 signed 2026-02-17 ([Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/passle/2026/02/alabama-becomes-the-fourth-state-to-enact-an-app-store-accountability-act)), though other firms give 2026-03-09. **UNVERIFIED**; the lawyer should confirm the act is law.

### California AB 1043 (Digital Age Assurance Act), which applies to developers too
- Operative **2027-01-01**. An operating-system provider must ask for a birth date or age at device setup and give apps an age bracket signal (under 13, 13-15, 16-17, 18+). A **developer** "shall request a signal" when the app is downloaded and launched; for apps already installed before 2027 it must request one before 2027-07-01. A developer that receives a signal is "deemed to have actual knowledge of the age range of the user ... across all platforms of the application and points of access". The signal is the primary indicator of age unless the developer has "clear and convincing" internal information that differs. The developer may not ask for more than needed or share the signal. Penalty up to $2,500 per affected child for a negligent violation and $7,500 for an intentional one, attorney general only. "Developer" is anyone who owns, maintains or controls an app, with no size test. **CONFIRMED** from the chaptered text, read on [CalMatters' Digital Democracy copy](https://digitaldemocracy.calmatters.org/bills/ca_202520260ab1043) because leginfo refused automated access; check it against leginfo.
- Signing date (reported 2025-10-13) and a pending amendment bill (reported as AB 1856) are **UNVERIFIED** ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/privacy-and-cybersecurity-law-blog/california-introduces-new-age-verification-requirements-for-software-applications)).
- Two consequences for OurSpace: the "actual knowledge across all platforms" language means a store signal received in the app would arguably bind the web version for the same person; and the Alabama and Louisiana texts tell developers to prefer the store's signal or the lower of two, so OurSpace's own birthday cannot be the only age source any more.

### What Apple and Google would require in practice
- **Apple.** The Declared Age Range API lets the app ask for an age category; the parent decides whether a child's age is shared. Apple's note of 2026-06-03 says new Apple Accounts in Texas are subject to age assurance from **2026-06-04**; developers should use the Declared Age Range API, the Significant Change API (PermissionKit), a new StoreKit `ageRatingCode`, and an App Store Server Notification to learn when a parent withdraws consent. Apple's age-assurance Q&A says that where a law requires it, "you must check the age of the people using your app", that this holds for 18+ apps too, and that developers decide what is a significant change. It requires the iOS 26.2+ SDK and, as of iOS 26.5, the API no longer says which verification method was used. Apple says developers should consult their legal counsel. **CONFIRMED** ([Texas note](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=sg176nne), [age assurance Q&A](https://developer.apple.com/support/age-assurance), [API reference](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/declaredagerange/)). Apple's pages do not address small developers or apps with their own minimum age.
- **Google.** The Play Age Signals API (beta) returns an age range (default 0-12, 13-15, 16-17, 18+; custom ranges can be set to the app's own minimum ages in Play Console), a status (verified, supervised, unknown and so on) and the source of the age. Google says it began returning signals for Texas users who created accounts after 2026-05-28, that it does not require developers to use the API, that developers must work out how the laws apply, and that Play will not disable an app because a parent refused a significant change. Use is limited to age-appropriate experiences in compliance with law, not advertising or analytics, and the information may be used only by the requesting app. Needs Android 6.0+. **CONFIRMED** ([overview](https://developer.android.com/google/play/age-signals/overview), [responses](https://developer.android.com/google/play/age-signals/understand-age-signals-responses), [Play Console help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/16569691)).
- **What this would mean for the code, in plain terms (no code written).**
  1. A native plugin for each platform. The apps are Capacitor shells and the web bundle cannot call either API, so the iOS shell needs a small Swift bridge to the Declared Age Range API (and PermissionKit's significant-change call), and the Android shell needs one to the Age Signals library.
  2. A rule for what the apps do with each answer: under 13 means refuse and discard the data; a teen whose store says no parental consent means block until consent; "not shared" or "unknown" needs a decision from counsel; consent withdrawn later means sign the person out. Apple's Q&A says Apple stops the app launching after a withdrawal; Texas and Utah still expect the developer's own system.
  3. A server endpoint (the Worker) for Apple's consent-withdrawn notifications, registered in App Store Connect.
  4. A way to reconcile the store's age bracket with the birthday the member typed, with the "lowest of the two" rule in Alabama and Louisiana, and an answer to "which age does Browse use". If a member typed an adult birthday but the store says 16-17, the teen/adult split in `src/browse/` has to use the lower age, which means Browse can no longer read the birthday alone.
  5. Deleting or not keeping the store's data: Texas s. 121.055(b) says delete it after verifying, and Utah and Alabama limit use. That sits oddly with a continuing check; counsel should say what may be stored (probably the yes/no result).
  6. A published, tested process for telling stores about significant changes before they ship: Texas counts a change in categories of personal data collected, new monetisation, or a material change to functionality as significant. Turning on the AI-detector vendor, adding push, or adding ads would each be a question.
  7. Age ratings and content descriptions entered honestly in both stores, with reasons (Texas s. 121.052). `docs/decisions/007-mobile-apps.md` already notes a social network with unfiltered user content usually rates 17+.
  8. The web site is not covered by any of the four app-store laws, because they cover apps from stores. A teen who uses the site in Safari gets none of this, which counsel may see as a gap or a relief. I did not find a source that says.
- **Current code against it.** Nothing in `mobile/` or `src/` calls either API. No server notification endpoint. No significant-change process. The age used everywhere is the typed birthday. **CONFIRMED** (repo search).
- **How sure.** Medium. The texts are clear that developers are covered and that no size exemption exists. What I could not establish is how hard anyone will enforce against a tiny free app, whether the Texas appeal fails, or whether an app that already bars under-13s and uses its own age gate has to do anything extra. Apple's FAQ reads as "yes, call the API wherever a law requires it" for a developer that is in scope.

## 2. COPPA and the amended rule

- **Does it apply.** COPPA covers operators of websites or apps "directed to children" under 13, and any operator with "actual knowledge" that it is collecting personal information from a child under 13 (16 CFR 312.3). Whether a service is directed to children turns on subject matter, visual content, child-oriented activities, music, audience evidence and marketing; the FTC also looks at representations and reviews (16 CFR 312.2). **CONFIRMED** ([eCFR part 312](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/part-312)). A "mixed audience" service that is not primarily child-directed may use a neutral age screen and apply COPPA only to people who identify as under 13; the screen must not default to a set age or encourage falsification (amended 312.2, per the [Federal Register notice](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule)). **CONFIRMED.**
- **FTC staff FAQ.** COPPA does not require a general-audience site to admit under-13s; a neutral screen lets people enter month and year freely; do not tell under-13s they cannot participate or encourage a different age; staff recommend a cookie so a child cannot back-button into another year; a site that later learns a user is under 13 (a parent's email, a post, a report) must get consent or delete the child's information and stop collecting; a service aimed at teens can still be child-directed if it attracts many under-13s; a site's terms banning children do not by themselves prevent a finding of child-directed. **CONFIRMED** ([FTC COPPA FAQ](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/complying-coppa-frequently-asked-questions), staff guidance, not binding).
- **The amended rule.** Published 2025-04-22, effective 2025-06-23; operators had until **2026-04-22** to comply with most changes (not 312.11(d)(1), (d)(4), (g)). The changes include a written information security programme, a written data retention policy, and separate verifiable parental consent before disclosing a child's data to third parties. **CONFIRMED** ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule), summary and compliance-date text read). They matter to OurSpace only if it becomes a service that has to comply, that is, after actual knowledge or if it is judged child-directed.
- **What it would require.** If COPPA applies: notice, verifiable parental consent before collecting from an under-13, parental review and deletion, security, and the new retention and third-party consent items. For OurSpace the realistic duty is the narrow one: do not keep collecting from, and delete, a child once you know.
- **Current code.** A neutral date field with no default, an age-block cookie, a refused sign-up that stores nothing in the account table (`ageGate` throws before `createUser`), and a report reason for "may be under the minimum age". Weak spots: (1) the message says "you have to be 13 or older", and the rules page and sign-up form show the threshold, which tells a child what age to type (the FTC FAQ discourages encouraging a different age; whether that wording is a problem is for counsel); (2) the cookie probably does not work in the apps; (3) a moderator can change a birthday on request, which could let a child fix the date; (4) nothing in `docs/operations.md` says what a moderator does after an "underage" report, how fast, or what is deleted. **CONFIRMED** (code read), with the app-cookie point UNVERIFIED.
- **Confidence.** Medium-high that COPPA is the "actual knowledge" kind for a service built this way. The risk is operational (what happens after a report), not the sign-up screen.

## 3. TAKE IT DOWN Act

- **Covered platform.** "A website, online service, online application, or mobile application that serves the public" and "primarily provides a forum for user-generated content, including messages, videos, images, games, and audio files". Email and broadband are excluded, as are services of mostly pre-selected content with incidental chat. **CONFIRMED** ([Public Law 119-12, sec. 4](https://www.congress.gov/119/plaws/publ12/PLAW-119publ12.htm)). A public social network with posts and photos looks covered. A small size, or the absence of revenue, is not an exclusion in the text.
- **Duties (sec. 3).** Establish a process for an identifiable individual (or an authorised person) to notify the platform of a non-consensual intimate depiction and ask for removal. The request is in writing and includes a physical or electronic signature, enough information to find the image, a good-faith statement that it was not consensual, and contact information. Post a "clear and conspicuous notice" of the process, in plain language, on the platform. On a valid request, remove the depiction "as soon as possible, but not later than 48 hours", and make "reasonable efforts to identify and remove any known identical copies". Good-faith removal is protected from claims. The FTC enforces; failure to reasonably comply is treated as an unfair or deceptive practice, and the FTC's reach extends to non-profit organisations. **CONFIRMED** (same source).
- **Compliance date.** The process had to be in place within one year of enactment (2025-05-19), so **2026-05-19**. **CONFIRMED** (same source). The FTC has said it enforces from that date; I did not re-read the FTC page. **UNVERIFIED.**
- **What the site needs published.** A page, linked from the footer and from the report dialog, that explains the process in plain language, says what a valid request must contain, and gives a way to send it that does not need an account. The statute also criminalises publishing such images (sec. 2), which `/rules` should probably say.
- **Current code.** The Moderation page starts a 48-hour clock for reports marked "intimate image without consent" and `docs/operations.md` says to remove, not hide, and remove identical re-uploads. **CONFIRMED.** Gaps: (1) reports are member-only (`AUTH` on `POST /api/reports`), but the person depicted need not be a member; (2) there is no public notice, no contact address and no form that gathers the signed statement the Act lists; (3) the 48-hour clock starts when a moderator marks the report "intimate", which may be later than when the request arrived; (4) DMs and private groups are not "published" in any ordinary sense, and I found no source on whether the Act reaches them; (5) "known identical copies" suggests keeping a hash of removed images, which the site does not do, and I did not verify what counts as reasonable efforts.
- **Confidence.** Medium-high that OurSpace is covered; low on how the FTC reads "reasonable efforts" for a tiny site.

## 4. Child-safety reporting: 18 U.S.C. 2258A and NCMEC

- **Who must report.** A "provider", defined as an electronic communication service provider or remote computing service (2258E). Once it obtains "actual knowledge of any facts or circumstances" showing an apparent violation involving child pornography, it must "as soon as reasonably possible" give NCMEC's CyberTipline its own contact details (address, phone, email, a point of contact) **and** make a report. Since the REPORT Act (Pub. L. 118-59, 2024-05-07) the triggers include section 1591 sex trafficking involving a minor and 2422(b) online enticement, not only images, which matters for DMs and live chat. A provider may also report planned or imminent violations. **CONFIRMED** ([2258A](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2258A), [2258E](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2258E); Cornell's copy of the Code).
- **What a small operator must not be forced to do.** No duty to monitor users, monitor communications, or "affirmatively search, screen, or scan" (2258A(f)). **CONFIRMED.**
- **Penalty for a knowing and wilful failure.** Up to $600,000 for a first failure by a provider with under 100 million monthly users, $850,000 for later ones (2258A(e)). **CONFIRMED.**
- **Preservation.** A completed report counts as a request to preserve "the contents provided in the report for 1 year" (changed from 90 days in 2024). The provider must also preserve visual depictions, data or files that are "reasonably accessible and may provide context or additional information about the reported material or person" (2258A(h)(2)), keep them "in a secure location" with access limited to what compliance needs (h)(3), and, from one year after 2024-05-07 (so since 2025-05-07), store them "consistent with" the NIST Cybersecurity Framework (h)(6). Longer, voluntary preservation is allowed (h)(5). **CONFIRMED.**
- **Who may act as reporter.** The statute puts the duty on the provider. NCMEC's technical documentation says API access needs a username and password "supplied by NCMEC", and the NCMEC XML receipt serves as the preservation notification under 2258A(h)(1) ([NCMEC API documentation](https://report.cybertip.org/ispws/documentation)). **CONFIRMED.** A NCMEC statement that unregistered providers can use only the public form and cannot attach images or videos came through a search snippet only (**UNVERIFIED**; see [NCMEC, "Exploitation Resources"](https://www.missingkids.org/gethelpnow/exploitation-resources)). Who is the "provider" for a project with no legal entity, and whether a contractor or a platform like Cloudflare could file for it, are questions I could not settle.
- **Limited liability.** A civil or criminal claim "arising from the performance of the reporting or preservation responsibilities" is barred unless there was intentional misconduct, actual malice, or reckless disregard (2258B). **CONFIRMED** ([2258B](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/2258B)). I did not research whether a moderator's own viewing and copying is protected.
- **Disclosure.** A provider may disclose reported material only to law enforcement, NCMEC or in response to legal process (2258A(g)(4)). **CONFIRMED.**
- **Current code.** Reports with reason `csam` go to the top of the queue, hide and hold the post's photos, and show a 48-hour clock. The moderator is told to report to the CyberTipline, then remove, then ban. Nothing is automated. **CONFIRMED.**

### Things in the repo's docs that look wrong or incomplete (flagged for the owner)
1. `docs/operations.md` says "**don't download, screenshot, copy or share it**" and, two steps later, tells the moderator to "keep a copy offline and off the site" with `wrangler r2 object get`. Those contradict each other, and a copy on a personal computer does not obviously meet the secure-storage and access-limit rules in 2258A(h)(3) or the NIST rule in (h)(6).
2. `operations.md`, `deploy.md` and `decisions/005` say "keep the report for a year". The statute is wider: the report contents **and** reasonably accessible context data (the account, related messages, other files) for one year after submission, stored securely. The docs describe only the photo and the report number.
3. "Report it to the NCMEC CyberTipline (report.cybertip.org)" is incomplete. The statute also requires the provider to give the CyberTipline its contact details, and NCMEC issues credentials to providers who report with content. Nothing in the repo says that has been done, or who the provider is. `decisions/005` question 7 already asks "Who is the CyberTipline reporter?"
4. `operations.md` says child-safety reports have "a 48-hour deadline". The 48 hours is the owner's own target (`TAKEDOWN_MS` in `src/safety/safety.js` says so). The statute says "as soon as reasonably possible" after actual knowledge. A 48-hour clock could be read as permission to wait.
5. The docs speak of images only. 2258A also reaches enticement and trafficking facts (2422(b), 1591) in DMs and chat, and OurSpace has no flow for reporting those. A `csam` report only holds the photos of a *post*; a chat message cannot be held the same way.
6. `docs/security.md` says the privacy summary on `/rules` should name Cloudflare's human check, the 90-day sign-up signals and the AI-detection vendor. The current `public/js/pages/rules.js` says only "a salted hash of your IP address, used only to stop spam (rate limits)". It also says deleting an account deletes "your page, posts, messages", while moderator-held photos survive an account deletion by design (`operations.md`). These are accuracy gaps in a privacy statement, and an inaccurate privacy statement is its own legal risk. **CONFIRMED** (both files read).

## 5. US state minor-protection and social-media laws

Status snapshot below is from one June 2026 law-firm survey ([Loeb & Loeb](https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/childrens-online-privacy-2026-state-app-store-design-code-and-social-media-laws)), so every line here is **UNVERIFIED** unless it says otherwise. Nothing was checked after June 2026 except where noted. I did not read these statutes, so I cannot say which have size or revenue thresholds that exclude OurSpace; several are written around large platforms.

- **California AB 2273, Age-Appropriate Design Code.** Covers a "business" (a legal, for-profit entity above CCPA thresholds) that offers an online service "likely to be accessed by children" under 18; the indicators include audience evidence and "design elements known to be of interest to children, including ... games, cartoons, music". On 2026-03-12 the Ninth Circuit vacated the injunction as to the coverage definition and the age-estimation requirement, affirmed it as to the data-use and dark-patterns restrictions on vagueness grounds, left the earlier DPIA injunction in place, and sent the case back. **CONFIRMED** ([opinion, No. 25-2366](https://cdn.ca9.uscourts.gov/datastore/opinions/2026/03/12/25-2366.pdf)). Whether a no-revenue project with no legal entity is a "business" is a question for counsel. **Sources disagree:** Loeb says the data-use provisions are now "likely enforceable"; the opinion says the injunction against them stands. NetChoice's own page says five of six challenged provisions remain enjoined. The opinion is what I rely on.
- **California SB 976 (addictive feeds).** In force in part: parental consent before a minor gets an "addictive feed" and private-by-default minor accounts; rehearing denied 2025-11-06 (Loeb, JURIST). Likely aimed at services with algorithmic feeds; OurSpace has a "For you" feed, so counsel should look.
- **Design-code style laws in other states** (Maryland, Connecticut, Colorado, Montana, South Carolina, Nebraska, Vermont, with dates between 2024-10-01 and 2027-01-01 in the survey). Several say "reasonably likely to be accessed by minors" and have no business-size threshold. Connecticut and Vermont are described as having none.
- **Parental-consent and age-verification social media laws.** Florida HB 3 (under-14 ban, consent for 14-15; the Eleventh Circuit let it be enforced), Mississippi HB 1126 (age verification for everyone; Fifth Circuit allowed enforcement; argued February 2026), Tennessee (injunction denied; Sixth Circuit argued February 2026), Nebraska LB 383 (effective 2026-07-01, challenged), Ohio (Sixth Circuit vacated the injunction 2026-06-18), Texas HB 18 SCOPE Act (enjoined, on appeal, with conflicting reports about a Fifth Circuit panel ruling and rehearing; I could not settle it), Utah, Georgia, Louisiana SB 162, Arkansas, Virginia SB 854 and Colorado (blocked at last report). Whether any of these reach a small service depends on thresholds I did not read; for example Mississippi's is described as covering "digital service providers" without a stated minimum.
- **Federal bills.** I did not check whether any federal app-store or social-media bill has passed. **UNVERIFIED (not checked).**
- **Confidence.** Low. This is the most volatile area, and the lawyer should start from a current tracker (NetChoice's litigation pages are one starting point) and from the statutes' coverage definitions.

## 6. EU and UK

### GDPR (EU)
- **Reach.** The GDPR applies to a controller outside the EU when processing relates to "the offering of goods or services, irrespective of whether a payment ... is required, to such data subjects in the Union" or to monitoring behaviour there (Art. 3(2)). A non-EU controller in that position must designate an EU representative unless processing is occasional and low-risk (Art. 27). **CONFIRMED** ([GDPR text](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)). A private social network with messaging is unlikely to count as "occasional".
- **Children.** Where consent is the legal basis for offering an information-society service directly to a child, the child must be at least 16, unless a Member State lowers it, but not below 13 (Art. 8). The controller must make "reasonable efforts to verify" parental consent. **CONFIRMED** (same source). Art. 8 bites only when consent is the basis; a service that relies on contract or another basis may be differently placed, and Art. 8(3) leaves national contract law untouched. Whether the "contract" route holds up for a minor is a counsel question.
- **Which age in which country.** The range is 13 to 16 and national laws vary. I did not obtain a verified table. A commercial list shows 13 for Belgium, Czech Republic, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, Latvia, Portugal and Sweden; treat that as a lead only ([Bird & Bird tracker, not read](https://childreninthedigitalworld.twobirds.com/home/eu)). **UNVERIFIED.**
- **Other GDPR items from the repo.** The sign-up signals (hashed network, ASN, hashed user agent), the stored birthday and the AI-detector vendor transfer all need a lawful basis, a privacy notice, a records-of-processing entry and an international-transfer answer. `docs/decisions/002` question 8 and `003` question 8 already ask about this.

### DSA (EU)
- **Reach.** Applies to intermediary services "offered in the Union", meaning a "substantial connection" through an EU establishment, a significant number of EU recipients, or targeting (Art. 3(d), with the recitals explaining the test). A provider outside the EU must appoint a **legal representative** in a Member State (Art. 13). **CONFIRMED** ([DSA text](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2065/oj)).
- **Small platforms.** Micro and small enterprises (Recommendation 2003/361/EC: under 50 staff and under EUR 10 million turnover or balance sheet, which I did not re-read) are exempt from the whole "online platform" section, which includes the minors-protection article (Art. 28) and the internal complaint-handling and trusted-flagger rules (Art. 19, except Art. 24(3)), and from transparency reports (Art. 15(2)). They are **not** exempt from the baseline: a single point of contact (Art. 11), a legal representative (Art. 13), terms and conditions that describe moderation, with a minor-friendly version if minors predominate (Art. 14), a notice-and-action mechanism (Art. 16), statements of reasons for removals, and notifying authorities of suspected serious crimes (Art. 18, listed in the contents as "Notification of suspicions of criminal offences"). **CONFIRMED** for Arts. 11, 13, 14, 15(2) and 19 (read); Arts. 16 to 18 I read only by heading, **UNVERIFIED** in detail.
- **Question mark.** Whether an unincorporated individual counts as a "micro or small enterprise" is not something I could settle.

### UK
- **Online Safety Act.** A user-to-user service is regulated if it "has links with the United Kingdom": a significant number of UK users, UK users as a target market, or it is capable of being used in the UK and there are reasonable grounds to believe there is a material risk of significant harm to people in the UK from user content (s. 4(5)-(6)). All regulated user-to-user services must do an illegal-content risk assessment, have content reporting and complaints, and keep records; those "likely to be accessed by children" must also do a children's risk assessment and protect children (s. 7). **CONFIRMED** ([s. 4](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/4), [s. 7](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2023/50/section/7)). Nothing in those sections sets a size floor.
- **Ofcom's guidance.** Ofcom says every user-to-user service had to complete a children's access assessment (deadline 2025-04-16 or three months after coming into scope), that a provider can conclude children cannot normally access it only if it uses "highly effective age assurance" plus access controls, and that most services without such controls are expected to be "likely to be accessed by children" ([Ofcom](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/online-safety/illegal-and-harmful-content/childrens-access-assessment-duties-under-the-online-safety-act)). **UNVERIFIED**: search snippets only, because Ofcom's site refused automated access. A birthday box is probably not "highly effective age assurance" under that guidance; counsel should confirm.
- **UK data law.** UK GDPR Art. 8 sets 13 as the consent age ([text](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2016/679/article/8)). **CONFIRMED.** The ICO's Children's Code covers information-society services "likely to be accessed by" under-18s and has 15 standards, in full effect from 2021-09-02, but I only have snippets ([ICO](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services/)). **UNVERIFIED.**

### The option of not offering the service in the EU and the UK
- What it would really take: (1) block sign-up and use by country at the Cloudflare edge (the Worker already reads `request.cf`, which carries a country), for the 27 EU states, the other EEA states and the UK; (2) remove those countries from availability in App Store Connect and Play Console (Google says Play does not change country targeting on its own and developers may add their own geo-restrictions); (3) say so in the terms; (4) decide what to do with existing members and with people who travel or use a VPN, since country at sign-up is not residence; (5) avoid anything that suggests targeting (EU languages, local marketing, an EU contact).
- **What blocking does not do.** It lowers exposure; it does not remove it. The DSA and GDPR tests look at whether the service is offered to or targets people there, and the OSA test includes "capable of being used in the UK" together with a risk of harm. Whether blocking at the edge, with a VPN gap, is enough is a legal question. **UNVERIFIED** as to how any regulator treats this in practice; nothing I read says a blocked small service is outside scope.
- I did not research Australia's under-16 social media minimum age (Apple's note of 2025-12-08 says it began 2025-12-10; [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=y1bckxf8)), Brazil's Digital ECA, or Singapore, which a non-US launch would also meet.

## 7. Section 230 and the DMCA

- **Section 230.** No provider or user of an interactive computer service "shall be treated as the publisher or speaker of any information provided by another information content provider" (47 U.S.C. 230(c)(1)). It does not limit federal criminal law, including chapter 110 (sexual exploitation of children), does not limit or expand intellectual property law, and does not bar certain sex-trafficking claims (230(e)). **CONFIRMED** ([47 U.S.C. 230](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/230)). It does not cover the app-store, privacy, TAKE IT DOWN or reporting duties above, which are the operator's own duties. Whether 230 reaches claims about the design of a service (feeds, recommendations, messaging between adults and minors) is a live question; I did not research the case law. **UNVERIFIED.**
- **DMCA safe harbour.** The limits on liability for user-posted material (17 U.S.C. 512(c)) apply only if the service provider "has designated an agent to receive notifications of claimed infringement", by making the details available on its website and giving them to the Copyright Office; acts "expeditiously" on notices; and has adopted, reasonably implemented, and told users about a repeat-infringer termination policy (512(i)). It must handle counter-notices (restoring the material 10-14 business days later unless the claimant sues). **CONFIRMED** ([17 U.S.C. 512](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/512)). Without the agent registration, the safe harbour is not available for photos and text members post.
- **Is an agent needed.** Nothing forces a site to register one, but 512(c)(2) makes it a condition of the protection. **CONFIRMED** (same source).
- **What it involves.** Register online with the Copyright Office; the current fee is $6 per designation, amendment or resubmission; the designation **expires after three years** unless renewed; the service provider must give its full legal name and a **street address (a P.O. box is allowed only on written request in exceptional circumstances such as a safety threat)**, plus alternate names (the app and site names) and the agent's name, address, phone and email; the same details must be on the site. **CONFIRMED** ([37 CFR 201.38](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/37/201.38), [Copyright Office FAQ](https://www.copyright.gov/dmca-directory/faq.html), [directory](https://www.copyright.gov/dmca-directory/)). The fee may change; check at the time.
- **Current code.** No agent, no DMCA page, no repeat-infringer policy, no counter-notice process, no public contact. The moderation tools (hide, ban) exist. With no entity, the "service provider" and its street address would be the owner personally, so the address would be public. That is a reason to settle the entity question first.

This table is a map for counsel, not a conclusion. "Burden" means more or fewer rules reach the service; none of these settings makes any of the rest safe.

| | `MIN_AGE` 13 (today) | 16 | 18 |
|---|---|---|---|
| App-store laws (TX, UT, LA, AL, CA AB 1043) | In scope. Teens 13-15 and 16-17 both exist as categories; store consent flows apply to both | Still in scope: the laws define "minor" as under 18 and still require checking age category. Utah lets a developer ask the store to block minors from downloading; Texas has no such step. Refusing every non-adult signal replaces the parental-consent work |
| COPPA | Excludes under-13s by a neutral screen; actual-knowledge duty | Same, and a larger gap between the minimum and 13 | Same duty if a child still gets in and is known |
